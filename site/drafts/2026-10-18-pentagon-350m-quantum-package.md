On October 8, the Department of War announced roughly $350 million in quantum computing initiatives: about $200 million in new DARPA funding and a conditional loan commitment of up to $150 million for PsiQuantum. The question this publication asks about every quantum announcement is the lender's question: who is the counterparty, what was promised, when is it delivered, what does it cost to finish, and what happens if it slips. Apply that here.

**The counterparty.** PsiQuantum is the best-capitalized photonic quantum company in the world: a $1B Series E in September 2025 at a $7B valuation, a $125M expanded DARPA agreement in July, and up to $100M in CHIPS incentives agreed in September. The proposed loan is not a rescue. It is a manufacturing bet: the money funds component and assembly test equipment, system-integration infrastructure, and advanced cryogenics for the company's 140,000-square-foot Milpitas hub, with PsiQuantum co-investing alongside. The collateral story, if you squint, is specialized manufacturing equipment for a machine that does not exist yet. That is the risk in one sentence.

**What "conditional" carries.** The Office of Strategic Capital's commitment is conditional on financial, legal, technical, and regulatory diligence. Four gates, any of which can kill or reshape the deal. The technical gate is the interesting one: it means the government's own engineers have to believe the photonic roadmap is buildable before the money moves. That is underwriting, not grantmaking.

**The milestone structure.** The $200M DARPA portion flows through the Quantum Benchmarking Initiative. On October 7, DARPA named Atom Computing, Diraq, IBM, and IonQ to Stage C, joining Microsoft and PsiQuantum in the final verification phase. QBI's stated objective is determining whether an approach can reach utility-scale operation by 2033, with computational value exceeding cost. Stage C's job is to verify whether the proposed systems can be constructed as designed and operated as intended. Read that as a milestone schedule: the government pays for verified buildability, not for roadmaps. Stage maximums run $1M (A), $15M (B), $300M (C), and the figures are ceilings, not guaranteed payments. More than $650M in prior DARPA investment sits underneath this.

**The credential signal.** A government stage advance works like a credit reference. It tells every other buyer and investor that independent reviewers examined a roadmap and found it credible enough to fund. That signal is part of what the money buys, and it is why stage announcements move news cycles out of proportion to the dollars.

**The credit-risk read.** Public quantum names are under real pressure: Rigetti down 21% in a month, D-Wave down 27.6%, Quantum Computing Inc. down 19%, while the broader market rose. Rigetti's Q2 showed revenue growth from Novera QPU sales but widening operating losses, with $541.3M in cash and no debt. It also holds a $100M Commerce/CHIPS R&D award. The pattern is consistent: government money is concentrating on a small set of private leaders (PsiQuantum's loan, the QBI Stage C cohort) while public-market investors discount the rest. If you underwrite this sector, the question is not whether the technology works in principle. It is which counterparties survive the valley between government validation and commercial revenue.

**What to watch.** Whether the OSC loan reaches final commitment and on what terms. What Stage C verification actually measures, and whether any participant fails it publicly. And whether the DOE's $215M fault-tolerant contest and the UK's £50M UKRI awards follow the same milestone-verification template. The template matters more than any single award: if governments converge on paying for verified buildability, that becomes the underwriting standard for the whole sector.
